================================================================================
 SCENARIO RECIPES : RESULTS & FINDINGS REPORT
================================================================================
 The README's "Scenario recipes" section lists seven generator variations plus
 a zone-crossing pointer.  Each recipe was run against the task it stresses
 most plausibly.  Reproduce everything with:

     python scenario_recipes.py


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 R1.  BUSY SINGLE SHIFT      generate(n_pickruns=500, n_days=1, seed=42)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 Relevance: this recipe is already the BASELINE of every task script (tasks
 1-4 all use it).  What it exposes is the DES regime split:

  ------------------------------------------------------------------
   release model                  | makespan (3 pickers) | aisle wait
  ------------------------------------------------------------------
   staggered (raw timestamps)     |      958.1 min       |   1.86 s
   synchronised (all at t=0)      |      419.6 min       |   4.41 s
  ------------------------------------------------------------------

 FINDING
   With raw timestamps the makespan is RELEASE-BOUND (pinned to the ~16 h
   release window) and almost nothing moves it — machine type, rerouting and
   solver choice all measure ~zero.  Synchronising releases to t=0 makes the
   system contention/throughput-bound.  This is why every task experiment in
   this repo syncs releases before measuring anything.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 R2.  LARGE WAREHOUSE        generate(n_aisles=20, n_positions=40,
                                       n_items=800, n_pickruns=1000)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 Relevance: Task 1 (solver comparison at scale) + DES scaling.
 800 locations, 10 one-way aisles.

  Solver comparison (200-run sample):        DES (nn routes, synced):
  ----------------------------------        -------------------------------
   nn          485.6 m     0.7s              3 pickers : 30.71 h  wait 5.2 s
   2opt        466.6 m     0.2s              6 pickers : 16.49 h  wait 15.2 s
   or_opt      467.2 m     0.2s
   aisle_nn    506.3 m     0.2s
   aco         464.7 m    28.7s
  ----------------------------------

 FINDINGS
   * Per-run distance roughly 2.6x the default warehouse (longer, more aisles).
   * ACO becomes the BEST solver at this scale (464.7 m, beating 2opt's 466.6)
     — bigger per-segment TSPs give the metaheuristic real headroom, consistent
     with the Task 1 conclusion that ACO gains grow with instance size.  Its
     runtime cost (28.7 s vs 0.2 s) remains the trade-off.
   * aisle_nn's S-shape sweep degrades relatively (+4% vs nn): sweeping all 20
     aisle columns is wasteful when picks touch only a few.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 R3.  SMALL DENSE WAREHOUSE  generate(n_aisles=4, n_positions=30,
                                       n_items=100, max_lines_per_run=15)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 Relevance: Task 1 (ACO headroom on long pickruns) and Task 2 (rerouting under
 maximum contention).  8.2 picks/run, only TWO one-way aisles (x in {1,3}).

 Task 1 angle (120-run sample):
  ------------------------------------------
   nn         259.8 m          2opt  256.8 m
   ACO base   254.2 m          ACO all 254.2 m
  ------------------------------------------
   ACO beats 2opt by ~1% here — more headroom than the default dataset (where
   it ties).  The three Task 1 improvements add nothing on top of base ACO in
   this instance; the colony already converges on the small 4-aisle graph.

 Task 2 angle — rerouting payoff, synced releases
 (% = makespan improvement vs no-rerouting baseline; NEGATIVE = worse):
  --------------------------------------------------------------
   pickers | baseline  | thr=5s   | thr=10s  | thr=30s
  --------------------------------------------------------------
      3    |  711.5 min|  -8.9%   |  -8.3%   |  -7.2%
      5    |  627.1 min| -20.9%   | -21.0%   | -20.9%
  --------------------------------------------------------------

 HEADLINE FINDING — REROUTING BACKFIRES IN A DENSE WAREHOUSE
   In the default 10-aisle warehouse (task2a) rerouting helped slightly
   (+0.4% to +1.2%).  Here it actively HURTS, by 7-9% at 3 pickers and a
   massive ~21% at 5 pickers.  Reason: with only two one-way aisles and most
   picks inside them, there is nowhere useful to go when blocked.  The picker
   defers the contested aisle, walks extra distance to the few remaining
   tiles, then returns — and the queue is still there (everyone else deferred
   and returned too).  Deferral adds travel without avoiding the wait.
   CONCLUSION: opportunistic rerouting needs ALTERNATIVE WORK to be a win; its
   value is non-monotonic in contention.  Low contention -> nothing to dodge;
   moderate contention with many aisles -> small win; saturated contention
   with few aisles -> active thrashing.  A production rule should check queue
   depth AND remaining alternative tiles, not just wait time.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 R4.  HEAVY-GOODS WAREHOUSE  weights -> uniform(200, 2000) post-generation
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 Relevance: weight-ordering (heavy->normal->fragile segmentation).
 Same transactions both runs (seed=1); only the weight CLASSIFICATION changes.

  --------------------------------------------------------------------
   weights        | class mix                      | avg dist | fixes
  --------------------------------------------------------------------
   default        | heavy 53% / normal 43% / fr 3% | 194.2 m  | 0.20
   heavy-goods    | heavy 81% / normal 19% / fr 0% | 195.5 m  | 0.56
  --------------------------------------------------------------------

 FINDINGS
   * Raw-order weight violations nearly triple (0.20 -> 0.56 fixes/run): with
     81/19 heavy/normal, a normal item slipping in front of a heavy one is a
     violation far more often than under the default mix.
   * Distance moves little (+0.7%): segment count per run changes only mildly.
   * (Caveat: reassigning weights post-generation does not re-slot inventory;
     locations keep their original weight_limit assignment.  Fine for the
     ordering experiment, but slotting-dependent experiments would need the
     weights changed inside generate().)


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 R5.  HIGHLY SKEWED DEMAND   Pareto shape 1.2 -> 0.5
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  --------------------------------------------------------
   shape | top-5% SKUs share of picks | top-20% share
  --------------------------------------------------------
   1.2   |           47%              |      75%
   0.5   |           96%              |      99%
  --------------------------------------------------------

 FINDING — RECIPE HAS NO ROUTING/DES EFFECT (generator limitation)
   The shape change produces the intended demand profile (a 95/5 split).  BUT
   the transaction generator draws pick items UNIFORMLY from inventory
   (data_gen.py: rng.choice(item_arr) — no probability weights), ignoring
   picks_30/60 entirely.  So demand skew changes the items TABLE but not which
   items actually get picked — routing and DES results are untouched.
   Natural extension: weight the rng.choice by picks_60 so fast movers
   dominate transactions; then skew would concentrate traffic on the fast
   movers' aisles and interact with slotting/contention.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 R6.  MULTI-DAY DEMAND WITH TREND   generate(n_pickruns=2000, n_days=90, seed=7)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  picks_trend over 300 SKUs: mean 0.39, median 0.40

  --------------------------------------------------------------
   thresholds            | accelerating | declining
  --------------------------------------------------------------
   README (>1.3 / <0.7)  |      3       |   273   <- misleading
   rescaled (>0.65/<0.35)|     45       |   129
  --------------------------------------------------------------

 FINDING — README THRESHOLDS DON'T FIT THE RATIO'S CONSTRUCTION
   picks_trend = picks_30 / picks_60, and picks_30 covers HALF the window of
   picks_60 — so steady demand gives a ratio of ~0.5, not 1.0.  The README's
   1.3 / 0.7 thresholds implicitly assume a normalised (rate-based) ratio and
   classify 91% of SKUs as "declining".  Halving the thresholds (0.65 / 0.35)
   gives sensible flags (45 accelerating / 129 declining).  Either rescale the
   thresholds or redefine the column as (picks_30/30)/(picks_60/60).
   Otherwise a data-profiling recipe — no routing/DES dimension (and per R5,
   transactions are not demand-weighted anyway).


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 R7.  COLD-CHAIN HEAVY       80% of slots reassigned to CHILLER/FREEZER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 Relevance: Task 4 — amplifies the cold-last constraint.

 FINDING FIRST — THE README SNIPPET AS WRITTEN IS A NO-OP
   The README reassigns ds.items["zone_type_1"].  But cold-last routing reads
   zones from ds.LOCATIONS (the loc_zone lookup in route_pickrun), and
   reassigning item zones after generation does not re-slot inventory.  So the
   snippet changes nothing observable.  Applied to ds.locations instead
   (same 80% / rng(3)/rng(4) construction):

  --------------------------------------------------------------------
   routing              | avg dist/run | avg ordering_fixes
  --------------------------------------------------------------------
   nn (no constraint)   |   190.2 m    |       0.08
   nn cold-last         |   211.6 m    |       1.24   (+11.3% distance)
  --------------------------------------------------------------------

 FINDINGS
   * With 80% of slots cold AND randomly interspersed, the constraint binds on
     nearly every pick: violations jump from 0.08 (weight-only) to 1.24/run.
   * The distance penalty is +11.3% — in line with task4b's interspersed
     result (+10.6% at 70/20/10), and the opposite sign of the default grouped
     layout (-2.2%).  Zone GROUPING, more than cold share, is what makes
     cold-chain compliance cheap.

================================================================================
 CROSS-RECIPE TAKEAWAYS
================================================================================
   1. Regime first: sync releases (R1) or nothing else is measurable.
   2. Solver gains scale with instance size: ACO goes from "ties 2opt" on the
      default dataset to outright best on the large warehouse (R2).
   3. Rerouting value is NON-MONOTONIC in contention: small win with many
      alternative aisles, ~21% LOSS when saturated with few aisles (R3).
   4. Three README artefacts found and documented:
        - R5: transactions ignore the demand columns (uniform item draw)
        - R6: picks_trend thresholds assume the wrong normalisation (~0.5 base)
        - R7: the cold-chain-heavy snippet edits items, but routing reads
          location zones — apply it to ds.locations
   5. Zone grouping, not cold share, drives the cost of cold-chain compliance
      (R7 vs task4b).
